## My six-stop presentation route

### 1. Target selection

I chose Arthur J. Gallagher & Co. (NYSE: AJG) because I worked there as a sales intern this summer and became interested in the insurance-brokerage industry. That experience made me want to better understand how a brokerage earns money and how growth through acquisitions affects the company. AJG is also a useful company to analyze because it has public filings, a clear commission-and-fee business model, a meaningful acquisition program, and qualified peers for comparison. My initial view was that reported growth alone did not establish value after a large acquisition. The main question became whether the AssuredPartners acquisition can create durable organic growth and cash generation that justify the added debt, integration costs, and acquired-intangible amortization.

### 2. Company and evidence

AJG earns insurance-brokerage commissions and fees plus risk-management and consulting revenue; it is not an insurer taking underwriting risk on its own balance sheet. I use **revenue before reimbursements**, because reimbursed expenses have an equal expense and are not an operating-growth driver. In FY2025 AJG reported $13,778m of revenue before reimbursements, $1,503m of net earnings, $1,396m of cash and cash equivalents, and $13,099m of gross corporate and other debt (USD millions; fiscal year ended December 31, 2025).

My primary source is AJG’s 2025 Form 10-K, filed February 17, 2026. The relevant statement pages are 70 (earnings), 72 (balance sheet), 73 (cash flows), 94 (debt), and 96 (EPS). The 10-K’s disclosed FY2025 organic base commission-and-fee growth was 6%, versus 20.7% reported total-revenue growth; that difference is why I do not carry reported growth straight into the forecast. The Q2 2026 earnings release supplies the more recent integration and financing context.

### 3. My pro-forma

The model forecasts FY2026E–FY2030E in USD millions and begins from the FY2025 10-K. I forecast organic revenue growth of 6%, 5%, 4%, 3%, and 3%, with no future acquisition spending in the base case. That is deliberately a stand-alone organic case after AssuredPartners, not a claim that acquisitions will stop. A named future deal would need its own purchase price, revenue, goodwill/intangibles, and financing assumptions.

The principal operating assumptions are compensation at 56.9% of revenue, operating expense at 16.4%, receivable days of 135.5, depreciation at 26.1% of opening PP&E, amortization at 8.6% of opening amortizable intangibles, flat $145m annual capital expenditures, a 19.7% tax rate, and $500m annual corporate-debt repayment. Cash is calculated after operations, working-capital changes, investment, debt repayment, and dividends; it is not plugged. FY2026E–FY2030E balance-sheet gaps are all 0.0 and cash remains above the $500m minimum without a revolver draw.

The economic logic is that brokerage growth contributes revenue at fixed modeled cost ratios, but it also requires receivable and other-working-capital investment. The base case makes acquisition spending visible at $0 rather than hiding unpriced acquisitions in a top-line growth rate.

### 5. Sensitivity and drivers

Lab 11 changes one independent input at a time, holding the rest of the FY2026E–FY2030E model at base. The base FY2030E result is $3,713.4m operating profit and $2,668.5m FCFE before dividends.

| Input and stated range | FY2030E operating-profit span | FY2030E FCFE-before-dividends span | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Organic revenue-growth path: base 6%, 5%, 4%, 3%, 3%; ±1.0 percentage point each year | $433.6m | $317.7m | Larger driver **over these selected ranges**. |
| Compensation / revenue: base 56.9%; 55.9%–57.9% in every forecast year | $338.4m | $271.8m | Material operating-cost driver. |

For the higher-growth case, the FY2030E path is: input growth increases revenue to $17,747.3m; compensation and operating expense rise at their fixed ratios; EBIT rises to $3,934.4m (+$221.0m); taxes and linked working-capital investment also rise; FCFE before dividends becomes $2,830.1m (+$161.6m). The FCFE increase is smaller than the EBIT increase because higher receivables/other operating assets and taxes absorb part of the contribution.

The ranking is not a probability statement and does not prove that growth is inherently more important than compensation. It depends on the ±1.0-point ranges chosen and does not model correlated changes. Because a defensible Lab 11 per-share valuation is unavailable, this sensitivity changes my research priority—verify organic growth and renewal/new-business evidence—rather than mechanically changing my valuation conclusion.

### 6. Interpretation

My supported conclusion is **watch-defer**. AJG has a strong, fee-based brokerage platform, but my stand-alone DCF and qualified-peer comparison are each materially below the September 3 market price, and FY2025 reported earnings include acquisition, integration, amortization, and peer-comparability qualifications. I would move toward initiate only if subsequent reporting shows durable post-acquisition organic growth and cash conversion, lower leverage, and consistently defined earnings that support the value. I would move toward do-not-initiate if integration, leverage, or repeated acquisitions prevent those outcomes.

My view changed from “revenue growth is encouraging” to “the source and cash conversion of growth matter.” The first evidence I would investigate is post-AssuredPartners organic-growth, retention, and new-business disclosure alongside debt reduction and acquisition/integration cash spending.

**Additional questions and partner responses:**

- **Model and valuation question:** “Please trace your higher-capex case from the cash-capex percentage through capex, FCFF, and per-share value. Which inputs are held fixed, and does the balance sheet still balance?”  
  **Response:** When cash capex as a percentage of revenue increases, cash capital expenditures increase. That lowers FCFF, which lowers the present value of forecast cash flows and the estimated share value. For this one-at-a-time sensitivity, the revenue-growth paths, EBITDA-margin assumptions, discount rate, terminal-growth assumption, and other base inputs are held fixed. The income statement, balance sheet, cash flow statement, and FCFF remain linked, and the accounting and liquidity checks pass.
- **Model and valuation question:** “Your cash-flow valuation is below the market price, but your peer comparison is higher. Why do those methods disagree, and why did you choose not to average them?”  
  **Response:** The cash-flow valuation uses assumptions about cloud and Productivity growth, EBITDA margin, capital spending, discount rate, and long-run cash flow. It is below the market price because those assumptions may be more conservative than the market’s expectations. The peer comparison is higher but relies on one imperfect peer. The methods were not averaged because they use different approaches and limitations; averaging would hide the disagreement rather than explain it.
- **Sensitivity and interpretation question:** “Capital spending was the biggest driver in your sensitivity. Could EBITDA margin become the biggest driver if you changed the tested ranges, and why?”  
  **Response:** Yes. Capital spending was the largest driver only within the selected ranges. A narrower capex range or wider EBITDA-margin range could make margin the larger driver. The ranking shows the model’s response to the selected ranges, not that capex is always the most important or most likely driver.
- **Follow-up question:** “If 81.3% of your enterprise value comes from the terminal period, what would make you reduce the terminal cash-flow or long-run growth assumption?”  
  **Response:** Later results showing that AI infrastructure is not producing enough cloud revenue or margin improvement to justify the spending, capex staying elevated longer than expected, or material cloud-growth slowing would support lower long-run cash-flow assumptions. Management guidance, weaker margins, or poor returns on new data-center capacity would be relevant evidence. Because 81.3% of enterprise value comes from the terminal period, that evidence could materially change the valuation and supports the watch/defer conclusion.

### My explanation back and feedback

**Explanation back:** Microsoft’s conclusion is watch/defer. Its cloud and productivity growth support the business case, but its valuation is highly exposed to the return on AI infrastructure spending: capital spending has the largest tested effect, and 81.3% of enterprise value is in the terminal period. Cash-flow valuations below market imply that the market expects stronger growth, profitability, or investment returns than the model supports. The peer estimate is not decisive because it rests on one imperfect peer.

**Strength:** The analysis does not force agreement among valuation methods. It explicitly identifies cash capex, EBITDA margin, and terminal-period dependence as the assumptions carrying the conclusion.

**Specific improvement:** Replace or supplement the single-peer comparison with a documented peer-selection policy and at least one additional suitably comparable company if the data permit. Also tie the 28%–23% capex path to a cited cash-capex definition and disclose the exact capex and margin sensitivity ranges. That would make the driver ranking and the claim about future investment returns easier to audit.
